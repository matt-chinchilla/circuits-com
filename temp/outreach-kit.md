# PHONE

## Before you dial

Say the company's own words back to them. If you can't say what they sell in one sentence, don't call yet.

Have the site open on the category you'd give them. If they go look while you're talking, that is the call going well, not badly.

If a call ends because you told the truth, it was never a founding partner.

---

## 1. The opener (20 seconds)

> "Hi, is this [Name]? — Matthew Chirichella, I'm calling from Lake Ronkonkoma. I built a components directory called Circuit Center, about five months in. I'm giving away exclusive placement in three categories to three companies, no cost, and I'm calling local first. Did I catch you mid-something, or do you have ninety seconds?"

Then stop talking. Let them answer.

**If they ask "what is this about" before you finish** — don't restart the pitch, compress it:

> "Free category-exclusive listing on a directory I built. I'm five minutes from you in Lake Ronkonkoma. Ninety seconds and you'll know if it's nothing."

**If they sound like they think you're selling ad space** — get ahead of it:

> "I'm not selling you anything today. There's no invoice at the end of this. That's the whole reason I can be blunt with you."

### Why this works — don't rewrite it

You lead with your own name and your own town. You state the offer inside the first fifteen words. You ask permission instead of assuming it. A telemarketer never says "did I catch you mid-something" because a telemarketer doesn't want the honest answer.

**Never say:** "How are you today." "I'll be brief" (then aren't). "Is now a good time" (invites "no"). Anything with the word "solution."

---

## 2. The ask (say it in one breath)

> "I want three real companies using this like a real company — your catalog, your part numbers, your customers clicking through. In exchange you get the category, exclusively, and I mean exclusively — the database only allows one company in that slot. Nobody else can occupy it while it's yours. And I want you to tell me straight where it falls short."

Then the catch, unprompted, before they ask:

> "Here's the part I should say up front, because you'd find it in about a minute anyway. Most of what's on the site right now is placeholder data. Only the Mouser rows come off a live feed. I'm not going to sit here and tell you it's finished. What I'm offering is the slot before it's worth anything, at a price that reflects that — which is zero."

## 3. The five objections

### "Never heard of you."

> "You wouldn't have. It's five months old and it's basically me — I've got three people helping. The site's live at circuitcenter.ai, you can pull it up while we're talking. Six thousand two hundred seventy parts across twenty-eight categories. I'm in Lake Ronkonkoma, Suffolk County. You can come look at it in person if you'd rather, I'm not far."

Then hand them the reason it's an advantage:

> "That's honestly why the offer is what it is. If I had a track record I'd be charging you. I don't, so I'm not."

Do not name-drop, do not imply momentum, do not say "we're growing fast."

---

### "What's the catch? What does it cost?"

> "Zero dollars, and I don't take a card. There's nothing on file, nothing auto-renews, no invoice shows up in ninety days. The way it works is you and I agree on what the site has to actually hit — traffic, clicks through to you, whatever number means something to you — and until it hits that, you pay nothing. When it does hit it, I call you and ask. You can say no. And whatever rate you come in at is locked for as long as you stay."

If they push — *"nothing's free"*:

> "The catch is you're betting your time on something unfinished. You'd have to send me your catalog and your part numbers, and you'd have to actually tell me when it's bad. That's real work on your end. That's the cost. I'd rather be straight about that than pretend there's no cost at all."

---

### "We already advertise on Digi-Key / Octopart / Findchips."

> "You should. I'm not asking you to move anything or cut anything — I'd be an idiot to ask you to leave a channel that works for a five-month-old site. This is additive and it's free, so the question isn't whether it beats Octopart. It's whether an exclusive slot that costs you nothing is worth a catalog file."

If they say those channels are expensive or crowded:

> "Then you already know the thing I'm trying to fix. On those you're one of forty listings on a part. Here you're the only company in the category, and that's enforced in the database, not by a salesperson's promise."

If they're on Digi-Key as a *supplier* rather than an advertiser — don't argue, redirect:

> "Different animal, understood. Then think of this as a channel you don't have yet rather than one you're comparing."

**Never** claim you're better than those platforms. You are not, and they know the market cold.

---

### "Send me an email."

Say yes — then get the second thing before you let go.

> "Happy to, I'll send it in the next ten minutes. Two things so it's not a waste of both our time — what's the best address for you directly, not the general inbox? And can I put fifteen minutes on your calendar for [Thursday] so you're not reading it cold? If you've read it by then and it's not for you, cancel it and I won't chase you."

If they won't take a meeting:

> "Fine. Then I'll send it and I'll call you back Thursday morning to hear yes or no. Is Thursday morning bad for you?"

That last question is the whole move. You're not asking for permission to call, you're asking them to veto a specific time. Most people don't.

If they still block it:

> "Understood. I'll send it, and I'll leave it with you — you've got my address in the signature, it comes to me directly, not a queue."

Then actually send it within the hour, with the subject line matching what you said on the phone.

---

### "I'd have to ask my manager."

Treat this as good news. It usually is.

> "Of course — I'd expect that. Let me make it easy to bring to them: it's free, there's no card, nothing renews, and it's exclusive to one company per category. There's genuinely nothing to sign. What's the one question they'll ask that I haven't answered for you?"

Whatever they say, answer it, then:

> "Two options. Either I send you something short enough to forward — one page, no attachment — or I get on with both of you for fifteen minutes and they can hit me with it directly. Which is less annoying for you?"

Then pin it down:

> "Who is that, by the way? And when do you usually get in front of them?"

Get the name and the day. Call back the day after.

## 5. Gatekeeper (reception answers)

Ask for the person by name, flat and unhurried, as if you've called before.

> "Morning — is [Name] around?"

**"What's this regarding?"**

> "Circuit Center — I'm in Lake Ronkonkoma. I've got an offer on category placement for [Company] I need to run past whoever handles sales. Should that be [Name], or is there someone better?"

That last half sentence is the important one. It turns the screen into a referral. If reception corrects you, you've upgraded the call.

**"Are you selling something?"**

> "The opposite, actually — I'm giving three companies free placement and I'm calling local ones first. If it's not for you it's a two-minute call."

**"Send it to info@."**

> "I will. Who reads that one, so I can put their name on it? And is [Name] the right person, or would it be someone else?"

**"He's not available."**

> "No problem. When's he normally at his desk — morning or afternoon? I'd rather catch him than keep leaving messages for you to pass on."

Be pleasant. Reception at a 631-number distributor has worked there fifteen years and knows everybody. Get their name and use it next time. They are the most useful person in the building.

---

## 6. What a good call actually looks like

**Nobody signs anything on this call. There is nothing to sign.** If you catch yourself trying to close, you've drifted.

**A win, in order of value:**

1. **A scheduled fifteen minutes with a date on it** — with the manager on it, if there is one.
2. **A named next step you own** — "I'll call you Thursday at nine," said by you, agreed by them.
3. **A referral to the right person** — the wrong contact naming the right one is a good call, not a failed one.
4. **A direct email address plus permission to call back** — not the general inbox.
5. **A clean no with a reason** — write the reason down. Three of the same reason means the offer is wrong, not the calling.

**A loss:** you talked for four minutes, they said "sounds interesting, send me something," and you hung up without a date or a direct address. That's a no with better manners.

**Set the follow-up while you're still on the phone.** "So I'm calling you Thursday at nine — put me in, I'll actually be there."

---

## 7. Close-out lines

**If they're in:**

> "Good. Here's what happens next: I'll email you today with the two things I need — your catalog file and the part numbers you most want in front of people. When those are in, I lock the category to you and nobody else can take it. And you'll have my cell, not a ticket queue."

**If they're a maybe:**

> "Fair enough. Three slots, and I'd rather you take one because you want it than because I wore you down. I'll call Thursday, you tell me yes or no, and either way I'll stop."

**If they're a no:**

> "Appreciate you being straight with me. One favor before I let you go — what would've made it worth ten minutes? I'm five months in and I'd rather hear the bad answer now."

Write down what they say. That answer is worth more than the call was.

## 3. Second DM — accepted, then went quiet (~1 week)

Send once. If nothing comes back, leave it there and try the phone instead.

> Following up once, then I'll leave you alone.
>
> The founding-partner thing is three companies, and I'd rather one of them be a Long Island shop than someone I've never met. If it's not a fit or the timing is wrong, saying so is genuinely useful to me — I'll take the no and stop.
>
> If it's just buried, my number's below and I'm easy to reach: matthew@circuitcenter.ai.

Two rules: say "once" and mean it, and make "no" an acceptable answer out loud. A second nudge that pretends the first didn't happen is what gets people marked as spam.

---

## 4. Warm-up comment on a prospect's post

Leave this a day or two *before* the connection request, on a post where they've said something substantive — a line card addition, a lead-time observation, a hiring post, a trade show recap.

> The lead-time point is the part most directories get wrong — a listing that doesn't say when it actually ships isn't much of a listing. Curious whether you're seeing that ease up on passives or just on the connector side.

**Why a comment like that lands rather than reads as spam:**

- It responds to one specific thing they said. Generic praise ("Great post!" / "Couldn't agree more") signals that you didn't read it, and everyone can tell.
- It contains no link, no company name, no offer. The comment's only job is to make your name familiar so the connection request three days later arrives from someone rather than from nobody.
- It ends with a real question they're qualified to answer. Sales people reply to questions about their own market; that reply is the first exchange, and it costs them nothing.
- It's short. A four-line comment on someone else's post reads as a hijack.
- Say something a person in the industry would actually say. If you don't know enough about the topic to have a view, skip that post and find another one — a comment that fakes expertise is worse than no comment.

Do this on two or three posts a day at most, spread across different people.

## 5. Realistic expectations

LinkedIn caps invitations at roughly 100–200 per week (Premium sits at the higher end, and the ceiling tightens if your acceptance rate drops), so hand-writing every note is not actually a bottleneck — you'll hit the platform's limit before you hit your own capacity. Personalised notes materially outperform blank requests; blank bulk requests are what triggers throttling and the "we noticed unusual activity" warning, and a run of ignored invites drags the limit down further. For cold founder outreach with a specific, local, non-salesy note, expect somewhere around 20–35% acceptance — better from inside sales reps than from VPs, better from people in Suffolk and Nassau than from anyone outside the area. Of those who accept, a minority will reply to the first DM at all: getting five or six real conversations out of a hundred invitations would be a good week, and you only need three partners. Track which of the three note variants gets accepted and change one thing at a time. Withdraw invites that sit unanswered for three or four weeks so pending requests don't pile up against your limit.

## Handling a company with several contacts

**FDH / BJG has eight names in the top fifteen, all behind one switchboard (631-737-1234).** That number is the trap. Asking the same receptionist for a different person every other day is exactly what spraying looks like from the inside, and the inside sales desk sits close enough to the sales leaders that a second call gets mentioned.

**One company, one conversation at a time.**

**Call Nathan Little first (VP of Sales).** He is local, he owns the sales number, and the title is senior enough to say yes to an exclusive placement without convening anyone — but he is closer to the day-to-day than the SVP, so he can tell you inside a minute whether the idea is useless. Ian Locke is the escalation, not the opener: go to him only if Nathan asks who signs off, or if Nathan goes dark after two attempts, and if so say plainly "I left two messages with Nathan and I'm not sure who owns this."

**Fallback after three business days, not three hours:** Brian Fox. Sales Manager, local, likelier to actually pick up a 631 call than a VP. Open with the truth — "I left a message with Nathan last week and I don't know who this belongs to" — which is honest and also inoculates you when the two of them compare notes.

**Do not call Janice Geyer and Anthony Zanetto.** Same title, almost certainly the same room; whichever one you skip will hear about it. Pick one, go there only after a sales leader has passed or gone silent, and go for feedback rather than the deal — "I'm not asking you to sign anything, I want to know if the part pages are usable."

**Joe Flora (Melbourne FL), Beth Lewandowski (DE) and Francine Franklin (FL) are a separate lane.** Different offices, different territories, unlikely to be in the Long Island hallway. You can work one of them in parallel with the local thread without it reading as spray — but still one at a time, and still tell whoever you reach that you also called New York.

**Same rule at the smaller shops.** Peerless: Wayne Wilson first, Jody Crouse only as a fallback (she is out of territory and further from the decision). Serendipity has four in the top fifteen — start with Vanessa Mendes as Executive Sales Manager, Jessica Cao second, and leave Caballero and Larkin alone unless both go quiet.

**Email discipline:** never CC two people at the same company, never BCC anyone, and never send the same paragraph twice inside one org. The plain test — if you would be uncomfortable with two of them forwarding your emails to each other, don't send the second one.

# DO NOT SAY

These apply on the phone, in email, on LinkedIn, and in anything you type into a chat window. A prospect who catches you on one number stops believing the honest ones. The candour is the product right now — protect it harder than the pitch.

- **Any figure describing how many of *their* parts are on the site.** You do not know it, it is not supportable, and they will check in minutes. The catalog-size column in the outreach queue is *their own* catalog size, not overlap — never read it out as coverage.
- **"We have customers already."** You have none. No customer count, no "a few companies are already on."
- **Any revenue, funding, traffic or growth number you have not measured and cannot show.** No "we're growing fast," no "traffic is up," no MRR.
- **"Everyone's signing up" / "spots are going fast."** Nobody has signed up.
- **"This offer expires Friday."** It doesn't, and they've heard it a thousand times.
- **Any claim the data is complete, live, verified, or accurate beyond the Mouser rows.** Do not call placeholder data "seed data," "sample listings that we're validating," or anything else that implies it is real. Say placeholder.
- **Any comparison claiming you beat Digi-Key, Octopart or Findchips at anything today.** Additive and free is the whole argument.
- **Anything beyond the stated offer.** The offer is: category-exclusive placement at no cost, founding rate locked for as long as they stay subscribed, $0 charged until mutually agreed KPIs are hit, no card on file, no auto-renew, a direct line to Matthew and a rep. No custom development, no guaranteed leads, no traffic promise, no exclusivity outside the one category slot, no timeline for when the placeholder data gets replaced unless you have actually committed to that date.
- **A commitment you cannot personally do that week** — including the day-4 email's offer to load their part numbers. If you can't, don't send it.
